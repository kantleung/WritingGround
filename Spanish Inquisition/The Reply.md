# Spanish Inquisition

You said, Spanish Inquisition, and then you said go. The room has already finished the thought. It is waiting for a line from a sketch, said with the napkins still in the lap. I am not going to say it. A sketch is a kinder evening than a tribunal, and shorter, and you asked for the tribunal.

What the phrase names is a court. Its title was the Tribunal of the Holy Office of the Inquisition. Pope Sixtus IV licensed it on the first of November 1478. Two years later Ferdinand of Aragon and Isabella of Castile named its first inquisitors, Miguel de Morillo and Juan de San Martín, and sent them south. On the fifteenth of July 1834 a decree finally abolished it, after two earlier abolitions had failed to stay abolished. Between those dates it was not a medieval ghost, and not a Spanish patent on cruelty. It was a royal office with a papal letter in the drawer. That is the fact worth keeping past the coffee.

The medieval Inquisition was a different machine. Rome had built it in the thirteenth century against the Cathars, and by the fifteenth it was moribund in Aragon, an old jurisdiction with little blood left in it. What the two crowns asked of Sixtus, and what he granted, was a court of heresy whose officers the monarchy would name, pay, and aim. The pope gave the licence. The crown kept the keys. People who know the subject from an engraving hear the word Church and sit back, as if the habit had been the explanation. The Church supplied the doctrine, and a great many of the men. The state supplied the will. An office that answers to a king will do a king's work, and call it the care of souls. Rome, later, built a congregation of its own. Portugal built a cousin tribunal. The judges in Galileo's case were not the heirs of this one. The popular memory marries all three, because a shudder prefers a single face.

Spain, in the year of the bull, was not a nation in any later sense of the word. It was a marriage. Ferdinand and Isabella had married in 1469, Castile and Aragon in one bed and two treasuries, with Granada still Muslim and the peninsula full of peoples the new court did not mean to leave as they were. The Jewish communities of Spain were among the largest in Europe, and a mob had already broken them. Not a pope, in 1478. A crowd, in 1391.

The killing began in Seville and ran. Synagogues burned. Baptism was offered with the street still wet, a surrender rather than a sacrament, and clerks entered it as a conversion. The children grew up inside the Church and inside a suspicion. The law called them conversos. The street had another word, taken from the pig, and used it as a stain. By the time Morillo and San Martín packed for the south, the converso was no longer a refugee from a massacre. He was a class. He kept the books, practised the medicine, married the cousins, farmed the taxes, and sat near the crown. Old Christians looked at the proximity and called it a plot.

They said the baptism was a mask. The Friday evening was still a Sabbath in all but name. The pork was pushed to the side of the plate. The dead were washed. The children were taught a silence. Some of this was true. Some of it was only the way a large family cooks for a hundred years after it has changed its public God. A court that sets out to detect the difference, and that takes a neighbour's word as the instrument, will find the heresy in the custom. It has defined the custom as the heresy. Ferdinand and Isabella did not invent the hatred. The towns had it, the cathedral chapters had it, the preaching orders had it in a purified and therefore more durable form. What the monarchs put on a salary was a procedure. A riot spends itself. A procedure recruits.

The first inquisitors reached Seville in November 1480. The heresy they came for was not a doctrine. Judaising: the crime of having been baptised, and of living, or of being said to live, as a Jew. Nobody was tried for being a Jew. The unbaptised stood outside the brief, which is why the crown, a little later, dealt with them by another instrument entirely. A Jew who kept the Sabbath was a Jew. A converso who kept it was a criminal. That distinction is the whole machine.

The numbers from those first years arrive shouting, and none agrees with the next. Bernáldez, the chronicler, put the burned in Seville at more than six hundred by 1488, and the penanced at five thousand. In 1515 the cathedral chapter, writing a statute to keep converso blood out of its own stalls, claimed six hundred burned and more than six thousand penanced since 1481. Zurita, the Aragonese historian, later wrote of more than four thousand burned and more than thirty thousand penanced by 1524. These are not counts. They are the noise a city makes when fear has begun to feel like virtue.

The paper, where it survives, speaks more quietly, and still not in a whisper. Béatrice Pérez, working through the Seville sentences down to 1524, found 4,924 of them, and 659 condemnations to death, 613 of those against the living. Klaus Wagner, counting notarial accounts of the public ceremonies up to 1524, reached 248 executions in 954 sentences. The distance between those modern figures, let alone between either of them and Zurita's four thousand, is the distance between a ruined archive and a legend that has already decided. I will not pick a favourite to please the table. In its first decades the tribunal at Seville killed in the hundreds, and humiliated in the thousands. Anyone who needs a larger figure is not short of sources. He is short of sources that agree.

Castile took the court because the crown pressed it in. Aragon was a harder mouth. It had its own laws, and the remains of the older inquisition, which at least was familiar. The new procedures offended local habit: witnesses kept secret, imprisonment that ran ahead of any charge the prisoner could answer, a file built towards a confession because the confession was what closed it. In 1485 an inquisitor was assassinated in the cathedral of Zaragoza. The method was murder. The message was constitutional. This was not a confessor with a terrible face. This was a change in who held the keys.

Tomás de Torquemada, the first Grand Inquisitor, gave the office the face it still wears. A convenience for illustrators, and a misfortune for the truth. He was a severe Dominican and a bureaucrat of unusual energy. A monster is irregular. Torquemada was a system, and after him the system no longer needed his zeal. The court was never a man. It was a staff.

A district was opened with an edict. In the early years it was an edict of grace. Come in, confess, name the others, and the penance will be lighter than the sentence. Grace meant a discount. It also meant a net. The man who came in to save himself was asked for names, and the names became the next file, and that file was opened with the same promise. This is how a city of hundreds of suspects becomes a city of thousands without a single new theology. Later the edict was of faith, which laid a duty to denounce. The neighbour who had seen clean linen on a Friday, or a candle lit at the wrong hour, or pork left on the plate, was not named to the accused. He might list his enemies and hope the judges were tired of them. He could not put her on oath in front of his eyes.

The question was not what he believed when the door was shut. The question was what he did with his hands. Did he work on Saturday. Did he eat the fat of the pig. Did he wash a corpse. Did the pan smell of olive oil on a day when a Christian pan smelled of lard. Belief was inferred from the kitchen. Custom survives baptism the way an accent survives a change of country, and a tribunal that reads the accent as a creed will never lack for business. It will occasionally be right, which is enough to keep a staff in conscience. The right cases are the ones an institution quotes. The others it files.

Torture was permitted. It was not the daily furniture the northern pamphlets sold, and it was not a rarity to be waved away with a reference to the age. The manuals go back to a handbook the Catalan inquisitor Nicolau Eimeric compiled in 1376, and they treat pain as a step in a process: a session, a notary, a surgeon, a limit. The limit was the pious part. A session could be suspended rather than ended, and resumed the next day as if a technicality had stopped the clock. I am not going to recite the methods. A dinner has no claim on them, and they are not the secret. Torture was not the punishment. The punishment was read out later, in public, under a sermon. Torture was how a court that hid its witnesses obtained the confession on which a hidden case could rest.

Then the ceremony the north heard as a bonfire, and the tribunal called an act of faith. The penitents came in the sanbenito, a yellow tunic, painted with a cross if the man was to be reconciled and with flames if he was not. On the head, a coroza, a mitre of pasteboard, so that a human being looked like a proposition in a sermon. Sentences were read until the voice gave out. The reconciled were fined, flogged, exiled, sent to the galleys, imprisoned, or returned to their streets inside a shame built to outlast the afternoon. Those condemned to die were relaxed to the secular arm. In its own theory the Holy Office shed no blood. It handed the man to the king's magistrate, who burned him, commonly the next morning, a little outside the theatre of forgiveness. Effigies burned for the dead and for the fled. A man who had died in prison could still attend, in pasteboard, and his heirs could still pay.

The distinction deceived nobody in the square. It is worth keeping because it shows the office's idea of itself: a court of mercy, with the state performing the killing the court had spent months arranging. Men believed a last confession could save the soul even when the body was lost. The sermon, the tunic, the magistrate, the stake: one event, divided by a form of words so that the division could be minuted. Bureaucracy loves a form of words. It sends every officer home with clean hands and a completed file.

The crown had a quieter interest than the soul. A heretic's goods were forfeit, and reconciliation itself could be expensive. A crowd spends its rage and looks for its dinner. A tribunal discovered that cases had a yield, and that familiars — lay officers, who wore the court's honour into their private quarrels — could cover a town. To be a familiar was a distinction, and a stick. The panic against the conversos thinned, as panics do, once the obvious victims had been burned, ruined, or frightened into a convincing conformity. The building remained. By the middle of the sixteenth century the machine had outlived the emergency that hired it. People born under it learned what not to say at table, and experienced the lesson as the way things were. That is how a tyranny prefers to be felt.

The nineteenth century took its figures from the tribunal's own secretary. Juan Antonio Llorente, writing when the office was already a patient, added up the Spanish tribunals from 1481 to 1808, left out the American courts and the Italian ones, and produced 31,912 people executed in person, 17,659 burned in effigy, and 291,450 penanced. Whether the century repeated him or denounced him, it started from his sum. Henry Charles Lea, who wrote the great hostile history at the beginning of the twentieth century, and who had no wish to spare the court, called those totals arbitrary. Then he declined to invent a better one. The early archive, he said, would not hold it.

The middle years will hold a count, with holes. In the 1970s Gustav Henningsen and Jaime Contreras went through the annual returns the provincial tribunals sent the Supreme Council, the relaciones de causas, for 1540 to 1700. They found 44,674 recorded trials, and 1,604 sentences of death: 826 against the living, 778 against effigies. They printed the gaps in the series. That has not stopped later writers from quoting the totals as if the gaps had been filled by wishing. Subsequent work pushed the number of trials towards ninety thousand, because a return sent to Madrid is not a complete local file. The executions did not rise with the files. In a mature tribunal, death was an uncommon sentence. Disgrace was the product. The court is misremembered as a furnace. For most of its life it manufactured penance, fines, shame, and paper.

Across the whole three centuries, serious historians still do not agree, and the disagreement is about thousands of dead. That is not a quibble. Henry Kamen, who has done more than anyone to sit the legend down in front of the archive, puts the executed at no more than three thousand, and the dead of the first forty years at about two thousand. Contreras, who had counted the middle years, told a television audience that the full toll lay between three and five thousand. Jean-Pierre Dedieu allows as many as ten thousand executions, four-fifths of them in the first thirty years, and something in the order of two hundred thousand trials. The fog is the founding generation, when the records were thin and the killing was thick. After that the record clears on a bureaucracy that did its worst early, and then spent two centuries policing a country it had already frightened into shape.

I am not handing you the smaller figure as a consolation. Three thousand people burned by warrant, or ten thousand, is a policy, not a rounding error in a pamphlet. The children of the reconciled, who lost the office and the marriage and the father's goods, and never saw a stake, are not restored by a footnote that corrects an engraving. The correction is about kind, not about kindness. The Spanish Inquisition was not a massacre on the scale its enemies needed for their wars. It was a permanent, lawful, minuted suspicion. It destroyed more people by infamy, prison, the galleys, exile, and confiscation than by fire. It taught a society to inform on its own kitchens. A massacre is easier to mourn, and easier to finish. A profession of suspicion has no natural end. It ends when someone abolishes the profession.

The expulsion of the Jews who refused baptism was the crown's decree, in 1492. It was not a sentence of the tribunal. I keep the distinction because the table will want to drop it. The ships and the files were twins, not the same hand. A monarchy that could not trust the Jew it had baptised decided it would no longer house the Jew it had not. Those who left were stripped of whatever a law can take without calling itself a thief. Those who stayed passed under this jurisdiction, and their grandchildren with them. After 1492 no legal Jew remained in those kingdoms to prove, simply by being there, that a man could decline the Church and still be a subject. Everyone was inside. That is a more radical thing than a bonfire, and it required a signature rather than a stake.

The converts from Islam walked the same road more slowly. Granada fell. Baptism was preached, then pressed, then treated as settled upon people who had not been consulted twice. They kept their baths, their Arabic, their calendar, and were denounced for all three, which is to say they were denounced for failing to become invisible. The tribunal worried at them for a century, under the name of moriscos. In the early seventeenth century the crown tired of the worry and expelled them. A people went onto boats for the heresy of having habits. If you want the portrait, do not paint only the Dominican. Paint the clerk and the king standing a little apart, each able to say that the other held the knife.

When the great heresies thinned, the office did not retire. Spain never had a Protestant public of any size. When Lutherans appeared, they were given ceremonies of unusual splendour, in the middle of the sixteenth century, in Valladolid and in Seville. A few dozen learned men, burned or reconciled in public, did the work of an announcement. The ordinary Tuesday was smaller, and the smallness is the late portrait. Blasphemy said in drink. Bigamy, pursued with a stamina out of proportion to any theological danger, because a double marriage can be proved from paper. A priest who used the confessional as a seduction. A tavern sentence, to the effect that to sleep with a woman was no mortal sin. A dream, repeated to the wrong friend. Orthodoxies, once they have won, do not fight ideas. They fine remarks.

There is one surprise, and a polymath is expected to produce them, so I offer this one because it is true and not because it balances a ledger. On the question of witches, the tribunal that burns in the European imagination was colder than the courts of the north. Panics did reach Spain. An inquisitor who had sat on one in the northern mountains, and was then sent back to look at it as a lawyer rather than as a frightened neighbour, reported that he could not find a witch. He found suggestion, fear, and questions that had taught the accused what to answer. Madrid, rather against the temper of the age, listened. The same office would still ruin a family over a pan of olive oil. What it had acquired was a professional scepticism about one particular craze, which magistrates in the German prince-bishoprics and in Scotland did not share. The pedantry saved lives. It is not an absolution.

The north had wars to fight, and a printing trade ready to serve them. The Dutch revolt, the English channel, the long fact of Spanish power: these required a moral portrait to hang beside the armies, and the Inquisition was already lurid enough to travel. By the seventeenth century a Protestant child could dread the name before he could have said whether Castile lay uphill of Aragon. The campaign later acquired a title, the Black Legend, and apologists have used the title as if an exaggeration were an acquittal. It is not. The legend inflated a real court. It also made Spanish cruelty a special cruelty, unlike the gallows at home, and therefore useful in any quarrel with Madrid. Torquemada became the face because a legend cannot use a committee. The Supreme Council was a committee. It sat, it minuted, it took the annual returns from a score of tribunals. A man dies. A minute-book does not.

There is a fashion now, the legend turned inside out, which finds that the dead were fewer than Llorente said and calls the affair mild. Mild is a word for weather. It is the wrong word for a court that concealed its witnesses, took the goods of the condemned, and hung the sanbenito in the parish church with the name attached, so that a town could read a family every Sunday for a generation. Fire ends on the day it is lit, which is why illustrators love it, and why it is incomplete as a measure. A name in a nave goes on blocking the marriage, the fellowship, the chair in the college. Statutes of purity of blood, which chapters and colleges were already writing before this tribunal existed, found in the Holy Office an ally with files. A font in one generation became a ban in the next. Spain organised descent as a qualification for trust. Later centuries would call the method modern, and forget where they had seen the cabinets.

The schoolroom keeps the fires of the 1480s, because fires are teachable. The survival is the more useful fact. In the eighteenth century the court tried less heresy of any grandeur and more superstition, more soliciting priests, more unguarded talk. It censored books. It obstructed ministers who wanted a tidier kingdom and kept tripping over a jurisdiction with its own revenues and its own pride. It could still be squalid in a particular case. It was no longer the terror that had emptied converso houses in Seville. It continued because continuation is the one skill an office possesses in perfection. Someone collects the wage. Someone else finds abolition slightly more trouble than neglect.

Abolishing it took three attempts. An institution that must be killed three times has ceased to be an idea about God. It has become property. Men drew salaries. Families placed sons. A reforming government could shut the door, and a restored monarchy could open it, because the door was still on the building and the paper was still in the cupboards. Paper waits out the reformer. The last decree is dated the fifteenth of July 1834. No procession marked the day. Someone with a pen found a moment, and the office stopped. It ended as offices end. Not in a convulsion. In a signature, filed.

You gave me a subject and a verb. The room expected a shudder it could pass round the table and then forget. The shudder is not counterfeit. It is aimed at the advertisement. The fire was how the tribunal announced itself to strangers, and to sketch-writers. The archive was how it lived, long enough to turn a policy into a climate. A state that sets out to own the inside of a mind will not, in practice, read the mind. The staff are not metaphysicians, whatever the seal claims. It will read the pan, the maid, the Friday candle, the sentence in the tavern, the grandfather's name on a tunic in a side chapel. It will call the reading a defence of the faith. It will cut a seal with a cross, a sword, and an olive branch, so that punishment and reconciliation appear to be one gesture, offered by the same hand.

The seal carried a line from the psalm, in the numbering the Church then used. Arise, Lord, and judge your cause. For three hundred and fifty years the cause was on the table every morning, in a council of six and in tribunals to the number of a score, and the Lord was not required to attend. The clerks had the cause. They were punctual. They kept the minutes. The minutes were the judgement.
