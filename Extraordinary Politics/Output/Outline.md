# Extraordinary Politics

*A history of shock therapy, in theory and in the world.*

Working outline for a single article of about 7,500 words (a thirty-minute read at a serious pace). If the draft runs long, cut first from the ancestors and from the ledger, never from Russia or from the theoretical core.

Research notes, with URLs and a record of what was not fetched, are in `../Research/`. The source log there governs any figure this outline has left conditional.

## The argument, in one paragraph

Shock therapy was a serious answer to a real trap: shortage, soft budgets, a monetary overhang, and a political window that would not stay open. It was not a conspiracy, and it was not a law of nature. Advocates bundled three operations — stabilisation, liberalisation, and a change of owners — and spoke of them as one dose. The dose did the narrow job, ending hyperinflation and queues, more often than the large job, founding a market society in a season. Poland is not the proof that the bundle works. Russia is not the proof that it fails. China is not the clean control the gradualists wanted. The article earns the right to say so only after it has kept the three operations apart.

## What this piece is not

- Not a prosecution in the manner of Naomi Klein, and not a defence brief in the manner of Anders Åslund or the later Jeffrey Sachs.
- Not a history of neoliberalism, and not a survey of the Washington Consensus. Those are neighbouring countries. Visit them; do not move there.
- Not a how-to, and not a moral. The pain was real. So was the alternative: queues, a printing press, and a state coming apart. Let both stand.

## Voice notes for the draft

British spelling. Narrative, not a literature review with names dropped like beads. Name a theorist when the idea cannot be separated from the person — Kornai, Balcerowicz, Sachs, Gaidar, Williamson — and otherwise let the argument carry the name. Irony is permitted at the medical metaphor, at John Williamson's loss of his own phrase, and at the gradualism offered to Western banks in 2008. It is not permitted at the unemployed, or at the dead. Where the record is contested, say so in the same paragraph as the claim. Do not park the dispute in a footnote the reader will skip.

Mark used below: **†** means the drafter must not freeze a digit, or a cause, without checking the series named.

## Opening — Warsaw, the week the queues died (~450 words)

**Purpose.** Put the reader in the wager before defining it.

**Beat.** Late December 1989: a Warsaw shop, empty in the socialist sense (the shelves are there; the goods are not). Early January 1990: the same street. Goods appear. Prices are savage. Someone who has queued for a decade is now priced out of what he can at last see. Hold both facts in one scene. Do not sentimentalise either.

**Turn.** The government called this a transformation programme. The street, and soon the press, called it shock therapy. The advocates preferred "radical reform" and "big bang." The medical nickname stuck, and it did illicit work: it cast a society as a patient, and pain as proof of cure. Say that once. Do not labour it.

**Promise.** The piece will follow the idea from a German weekend in 1948, through a Bolivian decree, into a theory assembled against a deadline in 1989, and then into the two tests that still govern the argument — Poland and Russia — with China standing nearby, awkward and indispensable.

## I. The wager (~600 words)

**Purpose.** Define the thing tightly enough that later chapters can show what was actually administered.

Shock therapy, in the sense that matters here, was a programme meant to be executed together, and at once:

1. **Stabilisation.** Close the budget gap. Stop monetising the deficit. Often, anchor the currency.
2. **Liberalisation.** Free prices. Free trade. Make the currency convertible for ordinary transactions. Let relative prices tell the truth after decades of not doing so.
3. **A change of owners.** Privatise, fast, before insiders finish the job informally.

The rhetorical bundle was the theory. The historical bundle was rarer. Bolivia stabilised a market economy in fiscal collapse. Poland liberalised and stabilised at speed, and privatised its large firms slowly. Russia freed prices into a monetary system the reformers did not control, and privatised in a hurry. Any sentence of the form "shock therapy worked" or "shock therapy failed" has already cheated.

**Theoretical stake, stated plainly.** Partial reform, the advocates said, was the worst of both worlds: state firms with a licence to lose money, prices that still lied, and a political class with time to organise against the next step. Speed was not cruelty added to economics. Speed was the economics. A credible regime change had to happen inside the short season when the old interest groups were stunned and the new ones had not yet formed. Leszek Balcerowicz would call that season extraordinary politics. The article takes the phrase as its title because the bet was political before it was mathematical.

**Distinguish, in a short passage, not a taxonomy:**

- Stabilisation shock (end a hyperinflation).
- Systemic shock (convert a planned economy).
- The Washington Consensus (John Williamson, 1989): a menu of policy reforms for Latin America, not a timetable, and not a theory of post-communist transition. Williamson spent later years complaining that his label had been stretched. One dry paragraph. Do not make him the villain or the sage.
- Klein's "shock doctrine": a political theory about disaster and opportunism. Note that it exists, that it uses the same words, and that it is a different argument. Do not debate it. The reader who wants that book knows where to find it.

## II. Ancestors: a weekend, and a dictatorship (~750 words)

**Purpose.** Show that the idea had a pedigree, and that the pedigree was already being asked to prove too much.

### The German weekend, June 1948

Ludwig Erhard, the currency reform, the Deutsche Mark replacing the Reichsmark, the lifting of price controls over a ruined market economy that still remembered how markets worked. The standard telling — Erhard informing the occupiers that he had not relaxed the controls, he had abolished them — may be used, labelled as the standard telling.

**What it seemed to prove.** Repressed inflation and empty shops can be ended in days if the money is replaced and prices are allowed to speak.

**What it did not prove.** That an industrial planned economy, with no residual market memory, no private owners, and no impartial courts, is the same patient. The illicit inference from Frankfurt to Warsaw is the seed of the later tragedy. Plant it here. Do not harvest it yet.

### Chile, 1975

The nearest Latin American ancestor, and a bad identity claim. Milton Friedman, on a visit in 1975, recommended a shock treatment against inflation. The Chicago Boys' wider programme — privatisation, trade opening, pension reform — ran for years under a dictatorship. It was not the conversion of a Soviet-type planned economy. It included a banking crisis in 1982, which is the first empirical rebuke, and it was imposed by force, which removes the very constraint (consent, elections, a free press) under which Balcerowicz would later claim that speed was necessary.

**Line to land.** Wikipedia and the popular record call Chile the first shock therapy. Resist that. Call it an ancestor. The difference between an ancestor and an instance is the difference this article is for.

**Optional aside, one sentence.** Latin American hyperinflation taught a generation of economists that speed could be mercy. Ending a price explosion in months is kinder than "gradual" disinflation that never arrives. That lesson was true. It was also portable in the wrong direction.

## III. Bolivia, 1985: the prototype that was not a transition (~600 words)

**Purpose.** Establish the proof that was real, and the syllogism that was false.

**The facts to use.**

- Supreme Decree 21060, 29 August 1985, President Víctor Paz Estenssoro. Jeffrey Sachs, then at Harvard, advising. This was the first place he could try the stabilisation argument on a living hyperinflation.
- The disease was monetary: a fiscal deficit financed by the printing press, in an economy that already had markets, private owners, and prices — mad prices, but prices.
- Do not freeze a peak inflation figure in the draft without the series in Sachs's own account and a second source. The safe claim is enough: hyperinflation of a severity that made the monthly rate the only number that mattered. It collapsed within months of the decree. The IMF and the World Bank returned.
- The social cost was not zero. State employment and real wages took the blow. Miners, in particular, paid. One concrete paragraph. No tableau of suffering; no dismissal of it.

**The syllogism, stated so the reader will recognise it later.** If a sudden fiscal and monetary break can end hyperinflation, then a sudden break can found a market economy. The first clause was earned in La Paz. The second was a change of subject. Poland would half-conceal the change, because Poland needed both operations. Russia would expose it.

## IV. The theory, assembled against a deadline (~1,150 words)

**Purpose.** The theoretical heart. Narrate it as an argument made in 1989–91 by people who thought delay had a body count, not as a survey of papers. Group the ideas; do not march through them chronologically unless the chronology is the point.

Open this section in Warsaw and Moscow in the late 1980s, not in a seminar. The old reforms — Hungarian goulash, Polish partial liberalisation, Soviet perestroika — had produced the same pathology: firms that could not fail, prices that did not clear, a monetary overhang waiting behind the queues, and a budget that leaked. János Kornai had already given this world its anatomy.

### The diagnosis they all shared

- **Shortage and the soft budget constraint** (Kornai). A state firm does not die. Losses are covered. Demand for inputs is therefore unlimited. Queues are not a temporary irritation; they are the equilibrium. Partial reform leaves the soft budget intact and adds a little market, which the soft budget then exploits.
- **Monetary overhang.** Years of forced saving and controlled prices meant that money in pockets exceeded goods at official prices. Liberalise slowly, and people flee the currency. Liberalise once, and the overhang is absorbed in a jump — brutal, visible, and finite. This is the German lesson, restated for a planned economy.
- **Credibility.** A gradual plan advertises its later stages to every lobby that will be hurt by them. The expectation of reversal is itself inflationary. A programme that happens on a Monday is harder to lobby than a programme scheduled for March. The lineage runs through the time-inconsistency literature, but the article should not stop for Kydland and Prescott. One sentence of ancestry is enough. The point is political economy: sequence is a target.

### Balcerowicz's addition, which is the article's spine

Extraordinary politics. After a regime falls, there is a short period in which the public will accept radical measures and the beneficiaries of the old order are too disorganised to block them. Then ordinary politics returns — parties, unions, firms, newspapers — and reform becomes a war of attrition. Therefore the painful measures must be taken together, at the start, inside the window. Speed is not an aesthetic preference for decisiveness. It is a claim about how long consent lasts.

This is the most serious idea in the whole story. Treat it with respect. Then note, later, the empirical catch: the window was used for different bundles in different countries, and the countries with a destination (Brussels) had a second window the theory did not name.

### The fight about order

Even among radicals, the dose was argued.

- **McKinnon and the sequencing camp.** Fiscal control and a tame financial system before full liberalisation. Freeing prices and trade in a country whose government cannot stop printing is not a therapy. It is a fire set to prove that water is useful.
- **The big-bang camp** (Lipton and Sachs, the 1990 Brookings paper on Poland; Balcerowicz's circle). Waiting for the right order is how the window closes. Stabilise, liberalise, and start privatising together, because each undone piece undoes the others.
- **Privatisation theory, which deserves its own pages inside this section.** Three methods: sell to the highest bidder (slow, clean, favours foreigners and those who already hold cash); give shares to insiders (fast, politic, locks in the old managers); give vouchers to citizens (fast, formally fair, governance-light). The argument for speed here was not efficiency. It was irreversibility. Boycko, Shleifer and Vishny would say it plainly for Russia: create owners, even mediocre owners, before a communist revanche can reclaim the factories. The counter-argument was already formed: mediocre owners with no courts, no banks, and no product market will strip the firm rather than run it. Spontaneous privatisation — theft by incumbents while the state dithers — was the cost of delay. Asset stripping was the cost of haste. The article should leave both costs on the table. This is the theoretical dispute the Russian chapter will cash out.

### Why output would fall, and why that fall was not, by itself, a refutation

The advocates predicted a recession. They did not, most of them, predict its depth. Give the reader the three mechanisms, in prose, not as a list of paper titles:

- **Credit crunch** (Calvo and Coricelli). Tight money stops inflation and also stops the working capital of state firms. They were addicted to soft credit. Withdrawal looks like a depression.
- **Disorganisation** (Blanchard and Kremer, a little later, but the idea belongs here). Planning had been a crude coordination device. Abolish it before contracts, suppliers, and reputations exist, and specific relationships break. Output falls because nobody knows who still delivers.
- **Reallocation** (Blanchard, and Kornai's transformational recession). Labour and capital must move from valued-by-planners to valued-by-buyers. The move is not instant. Unemployment appears in a country that had abolished it by decree, often by overmanning.

A fall in output is therefore not, on the theory's own terms, a verdict. A fall that deepens for years, in the absence of a new private sector, is. Teach the reader this distinction here. Use it without mercy in sections VI and VIII.

### What the gradualists were already saying, before the results came in

Do not present institutionalism as wisdom after the fact.

- **Peter Murrell**, evolutionary argument: a market is a set of routines, not a set of decrees. Importing the decrees overnight destroys routines that, however ugly, coordinated production. Better to grow new firms at the edge than to detonate the centre.
- **Dual-track theory** (Lau, Qian, Roland): liberalise at the margin, keep plan deliveries at frozen prices, and nobody is made worse off in theory. China was the existence proof they were formalising. The political version (Gérard Roland, and Dewatripont with Roland): partial reform can be the price of not being reversed.
- **The institutional line** that Stiglitz would later popularise, and that Douglass North had already made unavoidable: prices without owners, courts, and a central bank that will not print are new numbers on old power.

**Fairness rule for this section.** The radicals were not fools, and the gradualists were not wise men ignored. Each side named a failure the other side underweighted. Radicals underweighted institutions and coordination. Gradualists underweighted hyperinflation, the closing window, and the plunder available to insiders during a long transition. The article's judgement lives in that sentence. Do not soften it into "both sides had a point."

## V. Poland, and the neighbours who were not copies (~1,100 words)

**Purpose.** The empirical case that entered the textbooks as success. Recover its actual bundle, which is not the bundle of the slogan.

### The programme

Designed in the autumn of 1989 by Balcerowicz with Stanisław Gomułka, Stefan Kawalec, Wojciech Misiąg, and others. An outline was put before the public on 6 October 1989. Sachs was an influential outside adviser, and the Lipton–Sachs paper gave the programme its international theory. He was not the author of the Polish decree. Do not let the American name swallow the Polish one; the story is worse, and more interesting, if the choice was native.

Implemented 1 January 1990. Four instruments, from the Berg and Blanchard accounting:

- A fiscal swing toward balance, largely by cutting subsidies.
- A hard screw on domestic credit. The refinance rate for banks was set at 36 per cent *for the month of January*. Use that number. It does more work than an adjective.
- The *popiwek*: a tax penalty on wage bills that outran a norm. Prices were freed; wages were not. This is the detail that stops the cartoon of "they freed everything."
- Current-account convertibility of the zloty, at a fixed rate meant to anchor expectations.

### What happened, and what not to claim

- Shortages vanished. That part of the German lesson travelled.
- Inflation fell from the hyperinflationary monthly rates of late 1989, and did not become low. Victory over explosion is not victory over inflation. Keep the distinction visible.
- Output fell twice: once with stabilisation in early 1990, again in early 1991 when Soviet trade collapsed. The second shock was not made in Warsaw. Any account that treats 1990–91 as a pure test of Balcerowicz is cooking the experiment.
- Unemployment, unknown in official life, became ordinary and large. One paragraph on what that meant in a society that had treated a job as a condition of citizenship, not as a contract. No sermon.
- The private sector grew from a narrow base, much of it the small trade and services the plan had suppressed. State industry adjusted badly. Berg and Blanchard's question — whether the new sector could grow fast enough to replace the old — is the right question. Ask it in their words, then answer it with the next decade, not with 1991.

### The result that entered the argument

Poland was the first post-communist economy to climb out of the transformational recession and surpass its pre-reform output. On Balcerowicz's later accounting, GDP in 1999 stood about a fifth above 1989. Treat that as the architect's figure, consistent in direction with the independent record, not as a digit to tattoo. Growth in the second half of the decade was carried by the new private sector. The country also proved more resistant to the Russian crisis of 1998 than neighbours who had looked, on paper, similarly "in transition."

### The fact that wounds the bundle

Poland liberalised prices and trade at speed, and stabilised. It privatised large state firms slowly, messily, and often to insiders. The textbook success of shock therapy is a success of liberalisation and stabilisation with delayed privatisation. State this calmly. It is the hinge between the Polish chapter and the Russian one.

### The fast camp was not one camp

A short comparative passage, not a tour:

- **Czechoslovakia, then the Czech Republic.** Václav Klaus. Voucher privatisation, faster and cleaner on paper than Poland's, followed by a banking scandal and a quieter disappointment. Speed of formal ownership transfer did not equal speed of restructuring.
- **Hungary.** Already the most reformed socialist economy, and the most gradual in method after 1989: sales rather than a big bang, foreign owners rather than vouchers. Also a tolerable outcome. So Central Europe does not yield a simple moral in favour of speed. It yields a moral in favour of having a head start, a neighbour called Germany, and a destination called Brussels.
- **The Baltics.** The radical end, especially Estonia's currency board and clean break with the rouble. Small, new, willing to let the old industrial structure go. Useful as the limit case of extraordinary politics, not as a model Russia could have copied.

**Line to land.** If Poland proves anything transferable, it is that a hyperinflation can be stopped, queues can be ended, and a new private sector can outgrow a dinosaur — given a state that actually controls its central bank, a society of manageable complexity, and a political project (Europe) that made the pain legible. Those conditions are the story. The slogan is not.

## VI. Russia, 1992: the same words, a different patient (~1,250 words)

**Purpose.** The test that broke the slogan. Keep the operations separate or the chapter becomes a leading article.

### The patient

A planned economy far more militarised, far more monopolised, and far more thoroughly stripped of market memory than Poland's. A federation, not a nation-state with a single fiscal will. A rouble zone that survived the Soviet Union, so that fifteen treasuries could, in effect, print one money. A central bank that the reformers did not capture. An overhang and a fiscal collapse inherited from 1991, which Yegor Gaidar regarded as a gun already cocked. His case for speed — delay meant hyperinflation anyway — must be given in his own terms before it is judged. Read *Days of Defeat and Victory* for the temperature; do not subcontract the economics to a biographer.

### What was done on 2 January 1992

Price liberalisation, under Gaidar, with Yeltsin's political cover. Shelves filled. Savings died. Inflation did not stop, because the budget was not closed and the central bank (Viktor Gerashchenko is the name to use) continued to finance deficits and, through the correspondent accounts of the rouble zone, the deficits of others. Sachs's later claim is that this was not the Polish programme: the monetary anchor was missing, and the Western stabilisation fund that had backed the zloty was not forthcoming on anything like the same scale. State that as his claim. It is not nothing. It is also not an alibi for everything that followed.

**The sentence the chapter needs.** Russia ran a liberalisation shock without a stabilisation shock, and called the combination by the name of the full therapy. Critics who say the therapy failed, and defenders who say it was never tried, are describing the same gap from opposite ends.

### Privatisation, which is a second story and must feel like one

- **Vouchers, 1992–94.** The citizen as owner, briefly, on paper. Governance thin. Insiders dominant in practice.
- **Loans-for-shares, 1995–96.** A fiscal trick that became an ownership revolution. Auctions arranged so that the borrowers were the buyers. This created the oligarchs. It is not January 1992. A writer who blends the two has accepted the defendants' or the prosecutors' brief and should start the chapter again.
- The theoretical bet — mediocre owners are better than a reversible state — met the case the gradualists had feared. Without courts, competition, or a hard budget, ownership became a licence to strip. Say this as a mechanism, not as a sneer at "wild East" cartoons. The cartoons were produced by both Western correspondents and Russian oligarchs, for different reasons.

### Output

Official series show a deep and prolonged fall, on the order of two-fifths or worse from the late Soviet peak to the 1998 trough. **†** Do not print a single percentage. Alternative estimates, including Shleifer and Treisman's "normal country" argument, hold that Soviet output was overstated, the new private economy under-recorded, and the true fall therefore smaller — large still, but not a disappearance. The measurement fight is itself evidence: a statistical office built to count plans was a poor instrument for counting a collapse. Present the range. Then say what is not in dispute: the recession was longer and deeper than Poland's; recovery, when it came after 1998, rode an oil price the reformers had not planned on; and 1998 itself — default and devaluation — was the coda of a stabilisation that had never locked.

### Death

Male life expectancy fell sharply in the early 1990s, the blow concentrated on working-age men. **†** Confirm the series (Rosstat, WHO, Notzon and co-authors) before the draft names a year or a decimal. The fall is not a serious dispute. The cause is. Alcohol, a Soviet cohort already dying young, violence, the collapse of workplace medicine, and the economic shock are not rival slogans; they are candidate mechanisms, and the literature has not awarded the prize. Write one paragraph that a widow could read without feeling used, and that an epidemiologist could read without wincing. Do not write "shock therapy killed," and do not write "this would have happened anyway." Both are exits from the obligation to think.

### What Russia does and does not refute

- It refutes the casual transfer of the Bolivian and Polish stabilisation lesson to a state that does not control its money.
- It refutes voucher-plus-loans-for-shares as a way of creating capitalism rather than claimants.
- It does not refute the narrow claim that freeing prices ends shortages. The shelves did fill.
- It does not, by itself, prove that a slower Russian road was available in the winter of 1991. Gaidar's gun-already-cocked argument has to be answered, not waved away. The honest answer is that some stabilisation was unavoidable, and that the ownership shock and the monetary chaos were choices, including Western choices about aid and Russian choices about the central bank.

## VII. China: the control that is not a control (~750 words)

**Purpose.** Give the gradualist counter-example its full weight, then refuse it the status of a verdict on Poland.

**The path.** Household responsibility in the countryside from the late 1970s. Dual-track pricing in industry from the mid-1980s: plan quotas at plan prices, surplus at market prices. Township and village enterprises growing in the cracks. Special zones. No big-bang price liberalisation. No mass privatisation of state industry in the 1980s. The party remained the reformer after 1989, which is the opposite of extraordinary politics: there was no window, because there was no collapse. Deng's political order is not a detail. It is the condition of the economic path.

**The theory China forced the profession to write.** Lau, Qian and Roland: dual-track liberalisation can be Pareto-improving. Nobody's plan entitlement is confiscated; the market grows at the margin; coercion is not required for the gain. Barry Naughton's phrase, growing out of the plan, is the narrative version. Use it. Vietnam's *Đổi Mới*, from 1986, belongs in one sentence as the quieter cousin, not as a second case study.

**Why the comparison still cannot do the work asked of it.**

- China in 1978 was overwhelmingly agricultural. The surplus labour in the countryside was a resource Poland and Russia did not have. Their problem was overmanned industry, not peasants waiting to be allowed to trade.
- China had political continuity, a fiscal authority that held, and no Council for Mutual Economic Assistance to lose overnight.
- China had Hong Kong, Taiwanese capital, and a diaspora ready to walk through an open door. Russia had a diaspora of mathematicians.
- China postponed the politically lethal part — laying off the state-industrial working class — for twenty years, and then did it under a party that does not have to win an election. Citing Chinese gradualism as the road Poland should have taken is often a way of recommending a government Poland had just abolished.

**Line to land.** China shows that a market can grow without a therapy. It does not show that a hyperinflationary, highly industrial, politically collapsing system could have copied Guangdong. The gradualists won the right to be heard. They did not win a controlled experiment.

## VIII. The ledger, once there were enough years (~900 words)

**Purpose.** Leave the stories and say what the cross-country record can and cannot support. One section, late, so that regressions do not pretend to be narrative.

**The pattern that is sturdy.**

- Almost every Central and Eastern European and former Soviet economy suffered a transformational recession. China and Vietnam did not, in those years. The recession is a fact about exiting Soviet-type industry, not about one minister's temper.
- The recovery diverged. Central Europe and the Baltics returned to growth within a few years and, with the European Union as destination, converged in institutions as well as in income. Much of the former Soviet Union lost a decade. Resource exporters then followed oil, which is not a reform strategy.
- Where inflation was actually stopped, it stayed stopped more reliably than growth resumed. Stabilisation is the part of the programme with the cleanest empirical warrant. Systemic transformation is not a stabilisation with more courage.

**The pattern that is argued, and must be labelled as argued.**

- Early cross-country work (the World Bank, the EBRD indicators, de Melo, Denizer and Gelb, and the advocates' own regressions) found that faster liberalisation lined up with quicker recovery. Later work, Vladimir Popov prominent among it, argued that initial conditions, the trade shock, and distortions inherited from the plan explain more than the speed dummy. Mention the circularity once, dryly: the EBRD indicators measure policy adoption, and are then used to explain the outcomes of policy adoption. They are a useful scoreboard and a poor instrument.
- Privatisation *method* divides outcomes more cleanly than privatisation *speed*. Sales to outside owners, especially competitive sales, track restructuring better than insider giveaways. This is the empirical echo of section IV, and it vindicates neither the big-bang slogan nor the counsel of delay.
- The European Union is the variable the early theory underweighted. The *acquis* was an institutional import on a scale no IMF standby could mimic: courts, competition policy, a reason to suffer the *popiwek* that a citizen could explain to himself. Countries with a plausible accession path and countries without one were not running the same experiment. To call the difference "reform commitment" is to flatter a geography.

**Distribution.** Inequality rose, poverty rose and then, in the successful cases, fell, and a class of incumbents converted administrative power into property across almost every path — fast, slow, Chinese, Russian. The article should not imply that gradualism would have been egalitarian. It should say that the fast ownership shocks made the conversion sudden and, in Russia, grotesque, and that this had political consequences (cynicism about markets as such) which outlived the inflation.

**The verdict paragraph, which the whole piece has been earning.** Stabilisation worked more often than transformation. Speed of liberalisation is associated with recovery in some specifications and is crowded out by initial conditions in others. Institutions and starting points dominate the slogan. "Shock therapy failed" and "shock therapy was never really tried" are both too clean to survive contact with Poland and Russia held side by side.

## IX. Afterlives (~650 words)

**Purpose.** Show what the phrase became once it escaped the economists, and what of the original idea is still usable. Then stop.

- **Williamson**, briefly: a man overtaken by his own label. The Consensus was a description of what Latin American reformers and Washington institutions roughly agreed in 1989. It became a synonym for a timetable its author had not written. Useful as intellectual history in half a page. Do not relitigate the 1990s through his discomfort.
- **Stiglitz**, *Whither Reform?* (1999) and *Globalization and Its Discontents* (2002): the moment the institutional critique became the public verdict. Credit him with forcing governance, information, and sequencing back into a debate that had become tribal. Do not credit him with a superior reform that was ready to hand in Moscow in December 1991. He diagnosed the missing institutions more clearly than he supplied a political path to them.
- **Klein**, named and fenced, as in section I. One sentence here is enough, lest the afterlife chapter drift into culture war.
- **The Fund's own softening.** By the late 1990s the international institutions spoke more readily of safety nets, sequencing, and ownership of programmes. Record this as a change of language with some change of practice behind it. Do not award a conversion.
- **The irony of 2008**, one paragraph, understated. Western banks received capital, time, and a managed transition; "creative destruction" was explained as unsuitable for institutions to which the payments system was attached. Readers are capable of hearing the rhyme with the advice of 1992. Do not underline it.
- **Contemporary echoes, a short close, not a news peg.** Milei's Argentina is a conscious heir of the stabilisation shock, in a country with markets already, which puts it nearer La Paz than Moscow. Greek austerity was not shock therapy; do not conscript it. China's unfinished reform of state firms is the bill dual-track deferred. Use at most two of these. An afterlife chapter that becomes a survey of the present has lost its subject.

**Closing movement.** Return to the window, not to the shop. Extraordinary politics was a true description of a short season in several countries. It was a poor description of how a market society is built, which takes courts, habits, a central bank with a single sovereign, and a public that can lose an election without losing the regime. Shock therapy solved the problem it was designed for — monetary chaos and the queue — more often than the problem it was asked to solve. The asking was the error. The designing was not a crime.

The last sentence should be plain. No aphorism that asks to be quoted.
