# Figures checked for the article

Checked 7 October 2026, for the draft of *Extraordinary Politics*. A figure not in this note was not used.

## Bolivia

Juan Antonio Morales and Jeffrey D. Sachs, "The Emergence of Hyperinflation, 1982–85," in Jeffrey D. Sachs (ed.), *Developing Country Debt and Economic Performance, Volume 2* (University of Chicago Press for NBER, 1990). https://www.nber.org/system/files/chapters/c8938/c8938.pdf

- In the first half of 1985, inflation surged to an annual rate of about 26,000 per cent, approximately 60 per cent a month.

Juan Antonio Morales, "Inflation Stabilization in Bolivia," Documento de Trabajo 06/87, Universidad Católica Boliviana, July 1987. https://iisec.ucb.edu.bo/assets_iisec/publicacion/Inflation_Stabilization_in_Bolivia.pdf

- Hyperinflation, on Cagan's criterion, from about April 1984 to the first days of September 1985.
- In those seventeen months, prices rose by a factor of 623. Average monthly rate: 46 per cent.
- Sachs (1986) ranks the episode seventh in the twentieth century. The ranking is Morales's report of Sachs, not a table opened here.
- Programme of 29 August 1985. From March 1986 to March 1987, inflation was 21 per cent a year, or 1.6 per cent a month.

Morales and Sachs, "Ending the Hyperinflation, 1985–88," same NBER volume. https://www.nber.org/system/files/chapters/c8939/c8939.pdf

- Unveiled 29 August 1985, three weeks after Paz Estenssoro took office. Within days, the hyperinflation ended.
- A further jump later in 1985, tied to a large emission of money, was brought under control in mid-January 1986.
- The programme included trade and financial liberalisation, not austerity alone. Target deficit 6.3 per cent of GNP, of which 5.3 per cent to be financed from abroad. Tariffs unified at a flat 20 per cent.

Not used: the Wikipedia claims of a 93 per cent overnight devaluation and a $57 million IMF credit.

## Poland

Andrew Berg and Olivier Jean Blanchard, "Stabilization and Transition: Poland, 1990–91," in Blanchard, Froot, and Sachs (eds), *The Transition in Eastern Europe*, volume 1 (University of Chicago Press for NBER, 1994). https://www.nber.org/system/files/chapters/c6016/c6016.pdf

- Designed in the last quarter of 1989. Implemented 1 January 1990.
- Fiscal swing from a deficit of about 3 per cent of GDP in the last quarter of 1989 toward rough balance in 1990, mainly by cutting subsidies.
- Refinance rate for banks: 36 per cent for the month of January.
- Firm-specific wage-bill norm, only partly indexed, with penalties above the norm. No such restriction on prices.

"Poland — Macroeconomic Indicators for 1990–91," Library of Congress country study. https://countrystudies.us/poland/53.htm

Contemporary official accounting, as that study compiles it. Later statistical revisions were not fetched.

- Average price increase in the first month: 79.6 per cent.
- Annual price increases: 250 per cent in 1990, 70.3 per cent in 1991.
- Industrial output: −24 per cent in 1990, −12 per cent in 1991.
- GDP: −12 per cent in 1990, −8 per cent in 1991.
- Real wages (excluding agriculture and jobs financed directly from the budget): −29.2 per cent in 1990, +2.0 per cent in 1991.
- Real personal incomes: −22.3 per cent in 1990.
- That study's unemployment figures (6.1 per cent at end-1990, 11.4 per cent at end-1991) are not the ones used. Prefer the GUS yearbooks below.

Eurofound, "The dynamics of unemployment from 1990 to 2002," citing GUS statistical yearbooks. https://www.eurofound.europa.eu/en/resources/article/2002/dynamics-unemployment-1990-2002

Leszek Balcerowicz, "Poland's Transformation," *Finance & Development*, September 2000. https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/fandd/2000/09/balcerow.htm

## Russia

Vladimir Popov, "Internationalization of the Russian Economy: What Went Wrong?," *Macalester International*, vol. 7. Table 1, Russia, 1989–98. https://pages.nes.ru/vpopov/documents/Internationalization%20of%20the%20Russian%20Economy.pdf

His compilation of the official-style series. Not a Rosstat printout opened here.

- GDP, 1989 = 100: 96 (1990), 91 (1991), 78 (1992), 72 (1993), 62 (1994), 60 (1995), 57 (1996), 57 (1997), 53 (1998).
- CPI, annual average: 93 (1991), 1,526 (1992), 875 (1993), 307 (1994), 198 (1995), 48 (1996), 17 (1997), 50 (1998).
- Real personal disposable incomes, 1990 = 100: 63 in 1992, 65 in 1998.
- Gini, income: 26 in 1991, 39.8 in 1993, 40.9 in 1994.
- Gross fixed investment, 1989 = 100: 51 in 1992, 21 in 1998.
- Unemployment, year-end: 4.8 per cent in 1992, 12.5 per cent in 1998.

Michael Kaser, "Escape Routes from Post-Soviet Inflation and Recession," *Finance & Development*, June 1999. https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/fandd/1999/06/pdf/kaser.pdf

- In Russia, consumer prices rose sixteen-fold and producer prices twenty-fold in 1992 alone.
- In 1993, consumer prices rose 875 per cent in Russia, 4,085 per cent in Georgia, and 4,735 per cent in Ukraine.
- Inflation in the twelve CIS states slackened only after separate currencies were established.

Elizabeth Brainerd, "Winners and Losers in Russia's Economic Transition," *American Economic Review* 88, no. 5 (December 1998). https://web.williams.edu/Economics/brainerd/papers/aer98.pdf

- Overall wage inequality nearly doubled from 1991 to 1994, and rose above the American level.
- Women's wages fell relative to men's across the distribution.

David Walberg and co-authors, on the mortality crisis, as summarised from the *British Medical Journal* paper hosted at PMC. https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC28623/

- Life expectancy at birth fell between 1990 and 1994, and the fall varied widely by region of European Russia.
- The largest falls were in predominantly urban regions with high labour turnover, rising recorded crime, and higher but more unequal household income. Impoverishment is not a sufficient explanation.
- Deaths between the ages of 30 and 60 accounted for most of the fall, for women as well as men. Conditions linked to heavy alcohol consumption contributed most.

## The cross-country verdict

World Bank, *World Development Report 1996: From Plan to Market*, chapter "Liberalization, Stabilization, and Growth." http://www.rrojasdatabank.info/wdr1996/34-55.pdf

- Extensive liberalisation and determined stabilisation were both vital, and can and should be started early.
- Once annual inflation rises above a threshold of around 40 per cent, growth deteriorates dramatically.
- No transition economy in the discussion had registered sustained growth without containing inflation at moderate levels.
- Where growth came, it came largely from the lifting of restrictions on new entry, and from a surge of previously repressed activities, especially services and exports.

Oleh Havrylyshyn and Thomas Wolf, "Determinants of Growth in Transition Countries," *Finance & Development*, June 1999. https://www.imf.org/external/pubs/ft/fandd/1999/06/pdf/havrylys.pdf

- Their criterion is the sustainable recovery of output.
- Their conclusion, as the article's own summary states it: that recovery can be achieved only by controlling inflation and liberalising markets.
- Consistent-growth group, in their chart: Croatia, the Czech Republic, Hungary, Poland, the Slovak Republic, Slovenia, Estonia, Latvia, Lithuania.
- Little or no growth, in those years: among others, Kazakhstan, Moldova, Russia, Tajikistan, Turkmenistan, Ukraine.

Vladimir Popov, "Shock Therapy versus Gradualism Reconsidered: Lessons from Transition Economies after 15 Years of Reforms," *Comparative Economic Studies* 49 (2007): 1–31. https://carleton.ca/vpopov/wp-content/uploads/Shock-vs-grad-reconsidered-15-years-after-CES-2007.pdf

- Separate the transformational recession from the subsequent recovery, or the argument cheats.
- The collapse is best read as an adverse supply shock from the change in relative prices, given distortions inherited from the plan, plus the collapse of the state's capacity to enforce its own rules, plus macroeconomic mismanagement whether the packet was labelled radical or gradual.
- Speed of liberalisation, at that first stage, aggravated the fall if it did anything.
- At the recovery stage, ongoing liberalisation begins to pay. Inherited distortions fade. Institutional capacity and a sane macroeconomic policy remain necessary.
