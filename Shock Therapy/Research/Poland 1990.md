# Poland, 1990

Section V of the outline. The textbook success, recovered as a particular bundle: fast liberalisation and stabilisation, slow privatisation of large firms.

## The programme, from Berg and Blanchard

Designed in the last quarter of 1989. Implemented 1 January 1990. Four components:

1. Fiscal consolidation. The budget was to move from a deficit of about 3 per cent of GDP in the last quarter of 1989 to rough balance in 1990, mainly by cutting subsidies.
2. Control of domestic credit. Refinance rate for banks: 36 per cent for the month of January. Use this number. It is in the chapter, not in a memoir.
3. A tight incomes policy. A firm-specific wage-bill norm, only partially indexed to inflation, with heavy penalties for wages above the norm. No such restriction on prices. This is the *popiwek*. The chapter's point, which the article should keep: firms could adjust relative prices; they could not freely chase them with wages.
4. Convertibility of the zloty. The fetched pages break off as convertibility is introduced. Do not invent the rate, the reserve backing, or the degree of current-account restriction from memory. The outline's phrase "current-account convertibility, at a fixed rate meant to anchor expectations" is the standard account; confirm the rate and the reserve figure in Lipton and Sachs, or in the rest of this chapter, before the draft specifies either.

Berg and Blanchard's framing, which should govern the chapter: Poland was hit by two shocks. The first, early 1990, was the stabilisation. The second, early 1991, was the collapse of Soviet trade. State firms had a great deal of adjusting to do, and did not do it well. The private sector grew fast, from a narrow and uneven base. Their question — whether the new sector can grow wide enough to replace the old — is the right question for 1991. Answer it with the next decade, not with the trough.

Partial bibliography they themselves point to: Lipton and Sachs (1990); Gomułka; Coricelli and de Rezende Rocha; Lane; Berg and Sachs (1992); Schaffer (1992).

## The decade, from Balcerowicz in 2000

This is the architect's retrospect, ten years on. Right in direction, interested in tone. Treat the digits as his accounting.

- He dates the decision to transform to the break with the Soviet bloc, and the programme to 1 January 1990, under the name Economic Transformation Program. He does not, in this article, call it shock therapy.
- Poland was the first country in the region to rebound from the transformational recession and exceed pre-reform GDP.
- A moderate recovery in 1992–94, then the fastest growth in Central Europe in 1995–99, carried by the new private sector.
- On his figures, GDP in 1999 was 20 per cent larger than in 1989.
- He attributes later resilience — relative to the Czech currency crisis of 1997 and the Russian collapse of August 1998 — to balanced macroeconomic policy, structural reform, and a comparatively transparent financial system. The Russian crisis and an EU slowdown did hit Polish exports in 1999. Growth slowed to 1.7 per cent in the first quarter of 1999, then rebounded to more than 6 per cent in the fourth quarter of 1999 and the first quarter of 2000.
- The political frame he offers the IMF reader in 2000 is European integration, not the window of 1989. Useful for the ledger chapter: the destination had replaced the emergency as the public argument.

## Wiki, check before using

- The team that drew the plan in September 1989: Balcerowicz, Stanisław Gomułka, Stefan Kawalec, Wojciech Misiąg. **wiki**, and plausible. Balcerowicz's own F&D article, as fetched, does not name them. Gomułka is in Berg and Blanchard's bibliography. Kawalec and Misiąg should be confirmed in a Polish source or in Balcerowicz's book before the draft lists them as joint authors.
- An outline was presented to the public on 6 October 1989, at a press conference broadcast by TVP. **wiki.**
- The plan was "allegedly based on an earlier idea of Jeffrey Sachs." The word *allegedly* is the encyclopedia's, and it is the right caution. Sachs was an influential adviser; the Lipton–Sachs Brookings paper gave the programme its international theory. Do not write that he authored the Polish decree. Do not write, either, that the Poles proceeded without him.
- "There was a 3-year drop in output." **wiki**, crude. Prefer Berg and Blanchard's two shocks, and Balcerowicz's dating of the rebound to 1992.

## The hinge, which no fetched page states in one sentence

Poland liberalised and stabilised at speed. Large-firm privatisation was slower and messier than in Russia or the Czech Republic. That comparison is the article's, drawn from the standard record, not a sentence in the pages fetched this session. Before the draft leans on it, open a source that dates Polish mass privatisation against the Russian voucher programme and loans-for-shares. The hinge is too important to rest on recollection.
