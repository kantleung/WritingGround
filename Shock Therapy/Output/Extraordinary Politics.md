# Extraordinary Politics

*A history of shock therapy, in theory and in the world.*

---

In the last week of December 1989 a Warsaw shop was empty in the socialist sense. The shelves were there. The goods were not. People queued for what the plan had promised and the plan had not delivered, and they had been doing it long enough for the queue to feel like a fact of nature. A fortnight later the same street looked like a different country. Goods appeared. The prices attached to them were savage. A man who had spent a decade waiting for soap could at last see it, and could not afford it.

The government called this a transformation programme. The street, and soon the press, called it shock therapy. The men who designed it preferred other words: radical reform, the big bang, an economic transformation. The medical nickname stuck, and it did illicit work. It cast a society as a patient, and pain as proof of cure. A therapy is something a doctor administers to a body that cannot choose. Poland, in January 1990, was a democracy of six months' standing, and the dose was an act of politics.

The dose was also three operations sold as one. Stabilise the money. Free the prices. Change the owners. Bolivia had done the first, in an economy that already had markets. Poland did the first two at speed, and privatised its large firms slowly. Russia freed prices into a monetary system the reformers did not control, and changed owners in a hurry. China did none of these things in a week, and grew. Any sentence of the form "shock therapy worked," or "shock therapy failed," has already cheated. The history is the record of that cheat, and of the serious argument that preceded it.

## I. The wager

Shock therapy, in the sense that matters, was a programme meant to be executed together, and at once.

Stabilisation meant closing the budget gap, stopping the monetisation of the deficit, and, often, anchoring the currency so that people would believe tomorrow's prices. Liberalisation meant freeing prices, freeing trade, and making the currency convertible for ordinary transactions, so that relative prices would tell the truth after decades of not doing so. A change of owners meant privatising before the men who already ran the factories finished the job informally, by theft, by lease, by the quiet conversion of a ministry into a firm.

The rhetorical bundle was the theory. The historical bundle was rarer than the obituaries suggest. Advocates said that partial reform was the worst of both worlds: state firms with a licence to lose money, prices that still lied, and a political class with time to organise against the next step. Speed was not cruelty added to economics. Speed was the economics. A credible regime change had to happen inside the short season when the old interest groups were stunned and the new ones had not yet formed. Leszek Balcerowicz would call that season extraordinary politics. The phrase earns its place because the bet was political before it was mathematical. Consent, he argued, is a wasting asset. Spend it, or watch it spent by others.

Three neighbours of the phrase should be kept on their own side of the fence.

The Washington Consensus, as John Williamson set it down in 1989, was a menu of policy reforms that Latin American governments and the Washington institutions roughly shared: fiscal discipline, a competitive exchange rate, trade liberalisation, privatisation, deregulation, secure property rights. It was not a timetable. It was not a theory of how to convert a planned economy. Williamson spent later years complaining that his label had been stretched until it covered doctrines he had not written. The complaint is just. It does not make him either the author of the Polish decree or the villain of a morality play. A man overtaken by his own coinage is a familiar figure in the history of ideas, and a minor one here.

Naomi Klein's shock doctrine is a political theory about disaster and the uses to which it can be put. It uses the same two words for a different argument. The reader who wants that book knows where to find it. This one is about what the economists thought they were doing, and what the numbers, once there were enough years, were willing to say.

The third distinction is the one the rest of the essay exists to protect. A stabilisation shock ends a hyperinflation. A systemic shock tries to found a market society. They share a weekend, a metaphor, and very little else. Ending a price explosion in months can be a mercy. Founding capitalism in a season is a different claim, and a larger one than the evidence has ever supported.

## II. Ancestors

The pedigree begins, in the standard telling, on a weekend in June 1948. Ludwig Erhard, directing the economy of the western zones of occupied Germany, replaced the Reichsmark with the Deutsche Mark and lifted price controls over a ruined market economy that still remembered how markets worked. The anecdote in which he tells the occupiers that he has not relaxed the controls, he has abolished them, is the standard telling, and should be taken as such. What the weekend seemed to prove was real. Repressed inflation and empty shops can be ended in days if the money is replaced and prices are allowed to speak. Goods came out from under the counters. The German revival had other parents — Marshall aid, a skilled workforce, a state that held — but the weekend entered the economist's memory as a demonstration.

What it did not prove was that an industrial planned economy, with no residual market memory, no private owners, and no impartial courts, is the same patient. The illicit inference from Frankfurt to Warsaw is the seed of the later tragedy. It was planted in good faith. A generation of economists had watched controls produce queues, and had watched the lifting of controls produce goods. They were not wrong about the queues. They were wrong about how much of a society a price can carry.

Chile is the other ancestor, and a bad identity claim. In 1975 Milton Friedman, on a visit, recommended a shock treatment against inflation. The wider programme associated with the Chicago Boys — privatisation, an opening to trade, a new pension system — ran for years under a dictatorship. It was not the conversion of a Soviet-type planned economy. It included a banking crisis in 1982, which is the first empirical rebuke the idea received, and it was imposed by force. Force removes the constraint under which Balcerowicz would later claim that speed was necessary. A general does not need a window of consent. He has a different instrument. To call Chile the first shock therapy, as the popular record does, is to confuse an ancestor with an instance. The difference is the difference this history is for.

Latin America taught a lesson that was true, and portable in the wrong direction. Hyperinflation is not a policy disagreement. It is the destruction of the unit in which contracts, wages, and savings are written. Ending it in months is kinder than a gradualism that never arrives. Economists who had watched the printing press eat a currency were disposed to believe that delay was the cruel option, and that a sharp break was mercy. They were right about currencies. The error was to treat a planned economy as a currency with factories attached.

## III. La Paz

Bolivia, in 1985, was the place where that true lesson was earned in full.

The disease was monetary. A fiscal deficit was being financed by the printing press, in an economy that already had markets, private owners, and prices — mad prices, but prices. Hyperinflation, on the criterion Phillip Cagan had set down in 1956, ran from about April 1984 to the first days of September 1985. In those seventeen months, Juan Antonio Morales later calculated, prices rose by a factor of 623. The average monthly rate was 46 per cent. In the first half of 1985, Morales and Jeffrey Sachs record, the annual rate reached about 26,000 per cent, which is roughly 60 per cent a month. Sachs ranked the episode seventh in the twentieth century. The ranking is a measure of company, not of uniqueness. Bolivia had joined a short and miserable list.

President Hernán Siles Zuazo had already failed at the task. A last attempt in February 1985 produced a march on La Paz and a month-long sit-in by 10,000 miners. The government capitulated, called elections a year early, and gave up even the pretence of stabilising before a successor arrived. Víctor Paz Estenssoro unveiled the New Economic Policy on 29 August 1985, three weeks after taking office. Within days, the hyperinflation ended. The end was not a straight line. Later in 1985 prices jumped again, on a large emission of money, and that blip was brought under control only in mid-January 1986. From March 1986 to March 1987, inflation was 21 per cent a year, or 1.6 per cent a month. A currency had been pulled back from the absurd.

The programme was not austerity alone, which is the cartoon. Morales and Sachs are plain about this. It embraced a wide liberalisation of trade and finance. The exchange rate was unified and made convertible. Public-sector prices, especially for petroleum, were raised toward world levels. Public-sector wages were frozen. Tariffs were flattened to 20 per cent, and quotas went. Most price controls went with them. The target deficit was 6.3 per cent of gross national product, of which 5.3 per cent was to be financed from abroad. The Bolivian government believed, in the authors' phrase, "with considerable reason," that the liberalisation helped the stabilisation take hold. The dose was already being bundled. That belief is part of the road to Warsaw.

The social cost was not zero. State employment and real wages took the blow. Miners, who had brought down the previous attempt, paid in the coin of jobs. A history that mentions the 26,000 per cent and forgets the miners has chosen a side. A history that mentions the miners and forgets the 26,000 per cent has chosen the other. Both are available in the pamphlet literature. Neither is adequate.

Sachs advised the government. Bolivia was the first place he could try the stabilisation argument on a living hyperinflation. The success was real, and it was the wrong kind of success for the use that would be made of it. A sudden fiscal and monetary break can end a hyperinflation. From that sentence a second was inferred: a sudden break can found a market economy. The first clause was earned in La Paz. The second was a change of subject. Poland would half-conceal the change, because Poland needed both operations. Russia would expose it.

## IV. Assembled against a deadline

The theory was not born in a seminar and then applied to a country. It was assembled in Warsaw and Moscow in the late 1980s, by people who thought delay had a body count, against a state that was coming apart. The old reforms had already been tried. Hungarian goulash communism, the Polish liberalisations of the 1980s, Gorbachev's perestroika: each had produced a version of the same pathology. Firms that could not fail. Prices that did not clear. A monetary overhang waiting behind the queues. A budget that leaked. János Kornai had given this world its anatomy, and the anatomy was no longer controversial among people who had lived in it.

Shortage, in Kornai's account, is not a temporary irritation. It is the equilibrium. A state firm does not die. Losses are covered by the budget, by the bank, by a ministry that cannot contemplate a closure. Demand for inputs is therefore unlimited, because an extra tonne of steel costs the firm nothing it cannot have written off. Queues are what a soft budget constraint looks like from the pavement. Partial reform leaves the soft budget intact and adds a little market, which the soft budget then exploits. The firm sells what it can for hard currency, buys what it must at controlled prices, and sends the loss to the treasury. This is not a failure of nerve. It is the logic of an institution that cannot go bankrupt.

Behind the queues sat the overhang. Years of forced saving and controlled prices meant that money in pockets exceeded goods at official prices. Liberalise slowly, and people flee the currency as each announced step advertises the next. Liberalise once, and the overhang is absorbed in a jump — brutal, visible, and finite. This is the German lesson, restated for a planned economy. It is the part of the theory that travelled, and the part the Polish shelves confirmed.

Credibility supplied the political economy. A gradual plan advertises its later stages to every lobby that will be hurt by them. The expectation of reversal is itself inflationary. A firm that believes the controls will return does not restructure. It waits. A household that believes the currency will be confiscated again does not hold it. The literature on time inconsistency, Kydland and Prescott and what followed, had given this a formal dress. The dress is unnecessary here. The point is older than the formalism. A programme that happens on a Monday is harder to lobby than a programme scheduled for March.

Balcerowicz added the window. After a regime falls, there is a short period in which the public will accept radical measures and the beneficiaries of the old order are too disorganised to block them. Then ordinary politics returns — parties, unions, firms, newspapers — and reform becomes a war of attrition. Therefore the painful measures must be taken together, at the start, inside the window. This is the most serious idea in the story. It is a claim about how long consent lasts, not an aesthetic preference for decisiveness. It can be wrong, and in places it was. It cannot be dismissed as cruelty with a footnote.

Even among radicals, the order of the dose was argued. Ronald McKinnon, who had spent years on the sequence of liberalisation, held that fiscal control and a tame financial system had to come before the full freeing of prices and trade. Freeing prices in a country whose government cannot stop printing is not a therapy. It is a fire set to prove that water is useful. David Lipton and Jeffrey Sachs, in the Brookings paper of 1990 that gave the Polish programme its international theory, and Balcerowicz's circle in Warsaw, answered that waiting for the right order is how the window closes. Stabilise, liberalise, and start privatising together, because each undone piece undoes the others. The dispute was not between courage and cowardice. It was between two accounts of what delay costs.

Privatisation was a third argument, and it deserves to be kept apart from the first two, because Russia would later show what happens when it is not. There were three methods, and each had a constituency. Sell to the highest bidder: slow, clean, and a gift to foreigners and to those who already hold cash. Give the shares to insiders: fast, politic, and a lock on the old managers. Give vouchers to citizens: fast, formally fair, and silent on who will actually govern the firm. The case for speed here was not efficiency. It was irreversibility. Maxim Boycko, Andrei Shleifer, and Robert Vishny would say it plainly for Russia. Create owners, even mediocre owners, before a communist revanche can reclaim the factories. The counter-argument was already formed, before the results came in. Mediocre owners, with no courts, no banks, and no product market, will strip the firm rather than run it. Spontaneous privatisation — theft by incumbents while the state dithers — was the cost of delay. Asset stripping was the cost of haste. An honest history leaves both costs on the table. The pamphlet tradition picks one and calls the other a smear.

The advocates predicted a recession. Most of them did not predict its depth. Three mechanisms, worked out fully only as the numbers arrived, explain why a fall in output was not, on the theory's own terms, a verdict.

A credit crunch, as Guillermo Calvo and Fabrizio Coricelli described it, is what tight money does to a firm addicted to soft credit. Stabilisation stops inflation and also stops the working capital on which state industry had lived. Withdrawal looks like a depression because, for the firm, it is one. Disorganisation, as Olivier Blanchard and Michael Kremer later named it, is what happens when a crude coordination device is abolished before contracts, suppliers, and reputations exist to replace it. The plan was a bad way of deciding who delivers to whom. It was a way. Abolish it overnight, and specific relationships break. Output falls because nobody knows who still delivers. Reallocation is the kinder name for the same fact. Labour and capital must move from what planners valued to what buyers will pay for. The move is not instant. Unemployment appears in a country that had abolished it by decree, often by overmanning.

A fall in output is therefore not, by itself, a refutation. A fall that deepens for years, in the absence of a new private sector, is. The distinction is the one the Polish and Russian chapters will cash out. It should be learnt here, because both sides in the later quarrel forgot it when it suited them.

The gradualists were not wise men ignored. They were already speaking, before the results came in, and they named a failure the radicals underweighted.

Peter Murrell's objection was evolutionary. A market is a set of routines, not a set of decrees. Importing the decrees overnight destroys routines that, however ugly, coordinated production. Better to grow new firms at the edge than to detonate the centre. Lawrence Lau, Yingyi Qian, and Gérard Roland formalised the Chinese path into a proposition: liberalise at the margin, keep plan deliveries at frozen prices, and in theory nobody is made worse off. The market grows beside the plan. Coercion is not required for the gain. The political version, in Roland's work and in his papers with Mathias Dewatripont, was that partial reform can be the price of not being reversed. Douglass North had already made the institutional point unavoidable, and Joseph Stiglitz would later carry it to a public that does not read North. Prices without owners, courts, and a central bank that will not print are new numbers on old power.

The radicals underweighted institutions and coordination. The gradualists underweighted hyperinflation, the closing window, and the plunder available to insiders during a long transition. That sentence is the judgement of this essay. It is not a finding in any one paper. Both sides had a failure they could see in the other and not in themselves, which is the ordinary condition of a serious argument, and no reason to split the difference into mush.

## V. Warsaw

The Polish programme was not written in Cambridge, Massachusetts, and then cabled to a grateful province. It was designed in the autumn of 1989 by Balcerowicz and a small circle — Stanisław Gomułka, Stefan Kawalec, Wojciech Misiąg among them — and an outline was put before the public on 6 October. Sachs was an influential outside adviser. The Lipton–Sachs paper gave the programme its international theory. He did not author the decree. The American name has a way of swallowing the Polish one, which flatters a certain idea of how history is made. The choice was native. The advice was not irrelevant. Both facts can stand.

On 1 January 1990 four instruments came into force. Andrew Berg and Olivier Blanchard, taking stock two years later, set them out without romance.

The budget was to swing from a deficit of about 3 per cent of gross domestic product in the last quarter of 1989 toward rough balance in 1990, mainly by cutting subsidies. Domestic credit was screwed down hard. The refinance rate for banks was set at 36 per cent for the month of January. Not for the year. For the month. A rate of that kind is not a signal. It is a wall. A firm-specific tax on wage bills, the *popiwek*, penalised wages that outran a norm only partly indexed to prices. Prices were freed. Wages were not. This is the detail that kills the cartoon in which "they freed everything." They freed the thing that had been lying, and they held the thing that could chase it. The zloty was made convertible for ordinary transactions, at a fixed rate meant to anchor expectations. The rate and the reserve figure belong to a document this essay has not opened. The intention does not.

What happened is not what a slogan can hold.

Shortages vanished. That part of the German lesson travelled, and it travelled in weeks. Anyone who has not stood in a socialist queue is disposed to treat this as a small thing, a matter of shopkeeping. It was the daily experience of the economy. Its ending was the programme's clearest success, and the one most often omitted by critics who begin their story with the unemployment figures.

Inflation fell from the explosive monthly rates of late 1989, and did not become low. On the contemporary official accounting, compiled in the Library of Congress country study, prices rose by 79.6 per cent in the first month. The annual increase was 250 per cent in 1990 and 70.3 per cent in 1991. Victory over an explosion is not victory over inflation. The distinction matters, because the advocates' claim was the first, and their critics often prosecute them for failing at the second, as if a fever broken to a hundred were a fraud because it was not ninety-eight.

Output fell twice, which is the fact that cooks the experiment if it is ignored. Industrial production dropped by 24 per cent in 1990 and by a further 12 per cent in 1991. Gross domestic product, on that same contemporary accounting, fell by 12 per cent and then by 8. Later revisions of the path exist, and were not the series open for this essay; the direction is not in dispute, and the depth was greater than the designers had told the public to expect. The first fall belonged to the stabilisation. The second, in early 1991, belonged to the collapse of Soviet trade. Berg and Blanchard are explicit. State firms, which had a great deal of adjusting to do, did not do it well. The private sector grew fast, from a narrow and uneven base, much of it the small trade and services the plan had suppressed. Their question — whether the new sector could grow wide enough to replace the old — was the right question for 1991. It is the wrong question on which to close the account.

Unemployment, unknown in official life, became ordinary. At the end of 1990, on the GUS yearbooks, 1,126,100 people were registered, a rate of 6.5 per cent. A year later the rate was 12.2 per cent. At the end of 1993 it was 16.4 per cent, and 2.9 million people. Real wages, excluding agriculture and jobs paid directly from the budget, fell by 29.2 per cent in 1990. A society that had treated a job as a condition of citizenship, not as a contract, discovered the contract. The discovery was not a rounding error in a model of reallocation. It was the price of making firms mortal. To call it necessary is not to call it small. To call it a crime is to refuse the alternative, which was a printing press and a queue.

The decade that followed is what entered the textbooks, and it is real. Poland was the first post-communist economy to climb out of the transformational recession and surpass its pre-reform output. On Balcerowicz's later accounting, gross domestic product in 1999 stood about a fifth above 1989. Growth in the second half of the decade was the fastest in Central Europe, and it was carried by the new private sector, not by the resurrection of the old combines. The country also proved more resistant to the Russian crisis of 1998 than neighbours who had looked, on paper, similarly "in transition." Growth slowed to 1.7 per cent in the first quarter of 1999 and was back above 6 per cent within a year. Resilience is not the same thing as justice. It is not nothing.

The fact that wounds the bundle is quieter than the recovery, and more important. Poland liberalised prices and trade at speed, and it stabilised. It privatised its large state firms slowly, messily, and often to insiders. The textbook success of shock therapy is a success of liberalisation and stabilisation with delayed privatisation. State this calmly. It is the hinge on which the Russian chapter turns. If the slogan were true as a bundle, Poland would be a puzzle. Poland is a puzzle only for the slogan.

The fast camp was not one camp, and Central Europe refuses a simple moral.

Václav Klaus in Prague moved formal ownership faster than Warsaw, through vouchers that were cleaner on paper than Poland's delays. The vouchers were followed by a banking scandal and a quieter disappointment. Speed of transfer is not speed of restructuring. A share certificate is not a manager. Hungary, already the most reformed socialist economy in 1989, proceeded by sales rather than by a bang, and by foreign owners rather than by vouchers, and reached a tolerable destination by a different road. So the region does not yield a proof of speed. It yields a proof of something less portable: a head start, a neighbour called Germany, and a destination called Brussels. Estonia, at the radical end, with a currency board and a clean break from the rouble, is the limit case of the window. It is not a model a federation of 150 million people, with a military-industrial complex and no European Union to join, could have copied by an act of will.

If Poland proves anything that travels, it is narrower than the fame of the programme. A hyperinflation can be stopped. Queues can be ended. A new private sector can outgrow a dinosaur. The conditions were a state that actually controlled its central bank, a society of manageable complexity, and a political project that made the pain legible to the people who were paying it. Those conditions are the story. The slogan is the advertisement.

## VI. Moscow

Russia was not Poland with worse manners. It was a different patient, given the same prescription, and denied the instrument that had made the prescription work.

The economy was more militarised, more monopolised, and more thoroughly stripped of market memory than Poland's. The political unit was a federation, not a nation-state with a single fiscal will. The rouble zone survived the Soviet Union, so that several treasuries could, in effect, create one money. The central bank was not in the reformers' hands. Viktor Gerashchenko, who ran it, was not a man who believed that a shortage of credit was the problem. An overhang and a fiscal collapse had been inherited from 1991. Yegor Gaidar's case for speed, as he later set it out, was that the gun was already cocked. Delay meant hyperinflation anyway. The case has to be given in those terms before it is judged. A critic who begins from the oligarchs of 1996 and works backward has already decided that January 1992 was a choice among several calm alternatives. It was not calm. Whether it was the only available stabilisation is a different question, and a fairer one.

On 2 January 1992, prices were freed, under Gaidar, with Boris Yeltsin's political cover. Shelves filled. Savings died. Inflation did not stop, because the budget was not closed and the central bank continued to finance deficits — and, through the correspondent accounts of the rouble zone, the deficits of others. Michael Kaser, writing for the IMF in 1999, states the result without adjective: in Russia, consumer prices rose sixteen-fold in 1992, and producer prices twenty-fold. The annual-average consumer-price inflation that Vladimir Popov compiles for that year is 1,526 per cent, and 875 per cent in 1993, which is Kaser's figure for 1993 as well. These are different measures of one fire. They do not need to be blended into a single percentage to make the point. The point is that liberalisation arrived, and stabilisation did not.

Sachs's later claim is that this was not the Polish programme. The monetary anchor was missing, and the Western stabilisation fund that had backed the zloty was not forthcoming on anything like the same scale. State that as his claim. It is not nothing. A stabilisation in a country whose central bank will not stabilise is a press release. It is also not an alibi for everything that followed, and it has been used as one. The sentence the episode requires is plainer than either brief. Russia ran a liberalisation shock without a stabilisation shock, and called the combination by the name of the full therapy. Critics who say the therapy failed, and defenders who say it was never tried, are describing the same gap from opposite ends of the room.

Privatisation is a second story, and it must be felt as one. To blend it with the morning of 2 January 1992 is to accept a brief.

Vouchers, between 1992 and 1994, made the citizen an owner, briefly, on paper. Governance was thin. Insiders dominated in practice. The theoretical bet — mediocre owners are better than a reversible state — had not yet met its test. Loans-for-shares, in 1995 and 1996, was the test, and it was a different operation. A fiscal trick, in which banks lent the government money against shares in the firms the government could not bring itself to sell, became an ownership revolution. The auctions were arranged so that the borrowers were the buyers. This is where the oligarchs come from. It is not where the empty shelves were filled. A writer who needs a single villain for a single Monday has not looked at the calendar.

The bet met the case the gradualists had feared. Without courts, without competition, and without a hard budget, ownership became a licence to strip. Say this as a mechanism, not as a sneer at a wild east. The cartoons were produced by Western correspondents and by Russian oligarchs, for different reasons, and both had an interest in the picturesque. The mechanism is duller and worse. A man who can sell the firm's assets, park the proceeds offshore, and leave the workers with a wage arrear is not a capitalist in any sense Kornai or even Friedman would have recognised. He is the soft budget constraint in new clothes, with an offshore account where the ministry used to be.

Output, on the index Popov compiles, with 1989 as 100, stood at 78 in 1992 and at 53 in 1998. That is a fall of nearly half, over a decade, not a metaphor and not a one-year shock. Real personal disposable incomes, on the same table, fell from 100 in 1990 to 63 in 1992, and were still at 65 in 1998. Gross fixed investment stood at 21. The Gini coefficient moved from 26 in 1991 to 40.9 in 1994. Registered unemployment was only 4.8 per cent at the end of 1992 and 12.5 per cent at the end of 1998, which is lower than Poland's, and is not a comfort. Russian firms kept people on the books and stopped paying them. The queue had moved from the shop to the wage packet.

Shleifer and Treisman have argued, in a paper this essay did not have open, that Soviet output was overstated and the new private economy under-counted, so that the official fall flatters the disaster. The measurement fight is itself evidence. A statistical office built to count plans was a poor instrument for counting a collapse. The dispute may narrow the fall. It does not reverse the comparison with Poland, and it does not touch what happened to incomes in 1992. Recovery, when it came after 1998, rode an oil price the reformers had not planned on. The default and devaluation of 1998 were the coda of a stabilisation that had never locked.

Male life expectancy fell sharply between 1990 and 1994. The fall is not a serious dispute. A year and a decimal are, and this essay will not invent one. What the regional evidence will support is more useful than a national average in any case. David Walberg and his co-authors, looking at European Russia, found that the decline varied widely from region to region, and that the greatest falls were in some of the wealthier, urban regions. Impoverishment is not a sufficient explanation of a crisis that struck hardest where household incomes were higher. Deaths between the ages of 30 and 60 accounted for most of the fall in life expectancy, for women as well as for men, and the conditions that contributed most were those linked, directly or indirectly, to heavy drinking. Labour turnover, crime, and the inequality of income tracked the regional pattern. Alcohol, a Soviet cohort already dying young, violence, the collapse of workplace medicine, and the economic shock are not rival slogans. They are candidate mechanisms, and the literature has not awarded the prize to one of them.

Elizabeth Brainerd's companion finding, in the *American Economic Review* in 1998, is easier to state and no easier to live with. Overall wage inequality nearly doubled between 1991 and 1994, and rose above the American level. Women's wages fell relative to men's across the distribution. A market sorts. It also, in the absence of the institutions that make sorting something other than plunder, sorts by proximity to the old power and by the nerve to use it.

What Russia refutes is the casual transfer of the Bolivian and Polish stabilisation lesson to a state that does not control its money, and the use of vouchers followed by loans-for-shares as a way of creating capitalism rather than claimants. What it does not refute is the narrow claim that freeing prices ends shortages. The shelves did fill. What it does not prove, by itself, is that a slower Russian road was available in the winter of 1991. Some stabilisation was unavoidable. Gaidar was right about the gun. The ownership shock, and the monetary chaos that followed a liberalisation without a central bank willing to play its part, were choices — Russian choices about the bank, and Western choices about how much anchor to supply, and to whom. A history that collapses those choices into fate has stopped doing history.

## VII. The control that is not a control

China is the exhibit the gradualists deserved, and the verdict they have not been given.

From the late 1970s the household responsibility system returned the peasant to a residual claim on his crop. From the middle of the 1980s, dual-track pricing in industry did the thing Lau, Qian, and Roland would later write as a theorem. Plan quotas stayed at plan prices. Surplus moved at market prices. Nobody's entitlement was confiscated on the day the market appeared. Township and village enterprises grew in the cracks. Special zones opened a door that Hong Kong and Taiwanese capital were ready to walk through. There was no big-bang price liberalisation. There was no mass privatisation of state industry in the 1980s. The party remained the reformer after 1989, which is the opposite of extraordinary politics. There was no window, because there was no collapse. Deng Xiaoping's political order is not a detail of the Chinese path. It is the condition of it.

Barry Naughton's phrase is the narrative version of the theorem. China grew out of the plan. Vietnam's *Đổi Mới*, from 1986, is the quieter cousin, and one sentence is all it requires here. A market can be grown at the margin of a plan that is not detonated. The profession was forced to write that down, because the numbers would not permit the older story, in which only a bang produces a market.

The comparison still cannot do the work asked of it, and the asking is where a great deal of Western argument has gone to die.

China in 1978 was overwhelmingly agricultural. The surplus labour in the countryside was a resource Poland and Russia did not have. Their problem was overmanned industry, not peasants waiting to be allowed to sell a pig. China had political continuity, a fiscal authority that held, and no Council for Mutual Economic Assistance to lose overnight. It had a diaspora with capital. Russia had a diaspora of mathematicians, which is a gift to the institutes of the West and no substitute for a supply chain. China postponed the politically lethal part — laying off the state-industrial working class — for twenty years, and then did it under a party that does not have to win an election. Citing Guangdong as the road Poland should have taken is often a way of recommending a government Poland had just abolished. The recommendation is rarely made in those words. It should be.

China shows that a market can grow without a therapy. It does not show that a hyperinflationary, highly industrial, politically collapsing system could have copied the path. The gradualists won the right to be heard, which the big-bang camp had been disposed to deny them. They did not win a controlled experiment. There was no such experiment on offer, in 1989 or since.

## VIII. The ledger

Leave the stories. There are, by the late 1990s, enough years, and enough countries, for the cross-section to say something the anecdotes cannot. It can also say less than either camp claims. The institutional documents are more careful than their users.

Almost every economy exiting Soviet-type industry suffered a transformational recession. China and Vietnam did not, in those years. The recession is a fact about that exit, not about one minister's temper. Recovery then diverged. Central Europe and the Baltics returned to growth within a few years. Much of the former Soviet Union lost a decade. Resource exporters later followed the oil price, which is a geology, not a reform strategy.

The World Bank's *World Development Report* for 1996, *From Plan to Market*, is the document to read, not a later paraphrase of it. The chapter on liberalisation, stabilisation, and growth says both were vital, and that they can and should be started early. It states a threshold with more honesty than a regression usually permits. Once annual inflation rises above about 40 per cent, growth deteriorates sharply. High inflation obscures the relative prices that liberalisation has just gone to the trouble of revealing, and it inhibits the saving and investment on which a recovery would have to run. The negative result does more work than the positive one. No transition economy in the Bank's discussion had registered sustained growth without first containing inflation at moderate levels. Where growth came, it came largely from the lifting of the ban on new entry — services, exports, and, in Asia, agriculture — not from the resurrection of the old industrial ministries. The dinosaur did not learn to dance. A different animal occupied the ground.

Oleh Havrylyshyn and Thomas Wolf, writing in the IMF's *Finance & Development* in June 1999, are staff of the Fund, not a ministry's press office. Their criterion for success is the sustainable recovery of output. Their conclusion, as they state it, is that this recovery was achieved only where inflation was controlled and markets were liberalised. Their grouping is usable, and should not be pretended into a randomised trial. Consistent recovery, in their chart: Poland, the Czech Republic, Hungary, Slovakia, Slovenia, Croatia, and the three Baltic states. Little or no growth, in those years: Russia, Ukraine, and most of the rest of the Commonwealth of Independent States. The Fund's own scoreboard, nine years on, validates the narrow claim. It does not validate the bundle. Notice who is in the first group. It is, with few exceptions, the queue for Brussels.

Kaser, in the same issue, supplies the monetary corollary. The twelve states that stayed in the rouble zone imported Russia's price explosion. Consumer prices in 1993 rose by 875 per cent in Russia, by more than 4,000 per cent in Georgia, and by more than 4,700 per cent in Ukraine. Inflation slackened only after separate currencies existed. Stabilisation required a single sovereign over the note issue. That is an institutional finding. It is also the finding McKinnon had asked for before the experiment, and which the experiment then illustrated at continental scale. A liberalisation without a central bank is a phrase.

Where inflation was actually stopped, it stayed stopped more reliably than growth resumed. Stabilisation is the part of the programme with the cleanest empirical warrant, from La Paz through Warsaw to the Bank's forty-per-cent threshold. Systemic transformation is not a stabilisation with more courage added.

The dissent deserves the same courtesy, because it is not a mood. It is a specification.

Vladimir Popov, reconsidering the debate in *Comparative Economic Studies* in 2007, after a first statement of the argument in 2000, asks for a separation that both camps resist. Separate the collapse from the recovery, or the argument cheats. The collapse, on his reading, is an adverse supply shock. Deregulation changed relative prices. The plan had built a structure of industry and trade that those new prices could not support. A second shock arrived with the collapse of the state's capacity to enforce its own rules, so that transformation proceeded by crisis rather than by administration. Macroeconomic mismanagement made a third, and it did so whether the packet was labelled radical or gradual. Speed of liberalisation, at that first stage, aggravated the fall if it did anything. At the recovery stage the signs change. Ongoing liberalisation begins to pay. The inherited distortions fade. What remains necessary is institutional capacity, and a macroeconomic policy that is merely sane.

This is the sophisticated gradualist verdict. It does not say "do nothing." It says the slogan confused two periods, and then used the success of the second to bless the design of the first, or the pain of the first to condemn the second. Poland sits more comfortably in Popov's recovery clause than in his collapse clause. Russia sits in both, and indicts the macroeconomics more than it indicts the mere fact of having freed a price. A reader who wants a single moral will not get one from a man who has bothered to split the sample. That is a reason to read him.

One methodological sentence, and then done with it. The European Bank for Reconstruction and Development's transition indicators measure how much of the menu a government has adopted, and are then used to explain the results of having adopted it. They are a useful scoreboard and a poor instrument. The circularity is obvious once named, and it has not stopped the scoreboard from being treated as a cause. The World Bank's own later stock-taking inherits the problem. Name it. Do not build a theory on it.

Privatisation method divides outcomes more cleanly than privatisation speed. Sales to outside owners, especially competitive sales, track restructuring better than insider giveaways. This is the empirical echo of the theoretical dispute, and it vindicates neither the big-bang slogan nor the counsel of delay. Poland, slow to sell its large firms and quick to grow a new private sector, and Russia, quick to create claimants and slow to create firms that invest, are the two ends of that finding. The Czech vouchers sit awkwardly in the middle, a reminder that formal ownership is a legal fact and restructuring is an economic one.

The European Union is the variable the early theory underweighted, and the variable Havrylyshyn and Wolf's club mostly shares. The *acquis communautaire* was an institutional import on a scale no IMF standby could mimic: courts, competition policy, a product standard, a reason to suffer the *popiwek* that a citizen could explain to himself. Countries with a plausible accession path and countries without one were not running the same experiment. To call the difference "reform commitment" is to flatter a geography. Poland had a destination. Russia had a size. The destination did work the size could not.

Inequality rose on almost every path, including the Chinese, and a class of incumbents converted administrative power into property wherever the conversion was available. Gradualism was not a guarantee of egalitarianism. The fast ownership shocks made the conversion sudden and, in Russia, grotesque. Brainerd's doubling of wage inequality between 1991 and 1994 is the distributional face of the same years in which Walberg's regions were burying men of working age. The cynicism about markets as such outlived the inflation. That political consequence is not a residual. It is part of the result, and it is the part a growth regression is built not to see.

The verdict, which the stories and the documents now both support, can be stated without a trumpet. The World Bank and the IMF, writing in the late 1990s, validated the narrow claim. There was no sustained recovery, in their samples, without liberalisation, and none without inflation brought below the range where relative prices become noise. Popov, writing with fifteen years of data, validated the limit on that claim. Speed does not explain the depth of the collapse. Institutions explain the recovery better than the timetable does. Stabilisation worked more often than transformation. "Shock therapy failed" and "shock therapy was never really tried" are both too clean to survive Poland and Russia held side by side, and too clean to survive these two literatures held side by side. The first literature describes the conditions of a recovery. The second describes why a timetable is not a condition. A serious reader needs both, and will not be offered a synthesis neater than the countries.

## IX. Afterlives

The phrase escaped the economists, which is what phrases do when they are vivid and when the thing they name has hurt people. What it became is not always what it was.

Williamson, briefly. The Consensus was a description of an agreement he observed in 1989, among Latin American reformers and the institutions across the street from his office. It became a synonym for a timetable its author had not written, and then a term of abuse. A man overtaken by his label is entitled to his irritation. He is not entitled to rewrite the decade as a misunderstanding of his paper. The paper was a menu. Other hands turned it into a clock.

Stiglitz, in "Whither Reform?" in 1999 and in *Globalization and Its Discontents* three years later, is the moment the institutional critique became the public verdict. He forced governance, information, and sequence back into a debate that had become tribal, and he did it in prose a finance minister could be made to read. Credit the diagnosis. Do not credit him with a superior reform that was ready to hand in Moscow in December 1991. He saw the missing institutions more clearly than he supplied a political path to them. Institutions are not a policy instrument in the way a refinance rate is. They are the accumulation of the thing the rate cannot buy. To say so in 1999 was necessary. To imply that Gaidar could have ordered them up in a decree is the gradualist version of the original error, which was to believe that the important things happen on a Monday.

Klein's argument remains a different book. One sentence was enough at the start. It is enough here.

The Fund's own language softened. By the late 1990s the international institutions spoke more readily of safety nets, of sequencing, of the "ownership" of programmes by the governments that would have to defend them. Havrylyshyn and Wolf are themselves a specimen of the shift: success defined as a recovery of output, not as a checklist of liberalisations completed. Record this as a change of language with some change of practice behind it. Do not award a conversion. Institutions do not convert. They absorb the last disaster into the next guideline, and keep their acronym.

There is a rhyme in 2008, and it should be left as a rhyme. Western banks, in the autumn of that year, received capital, time, and a managed transition. Creative destruction was explained as unsuitable for institutions to which the payments system was attached. The explanation was not absurd. A payments system is not a shoe factory, and letting it fail in order to improve the managers is a policy with a body count of its own. Readers are capable of hearing the echo of 1992, when a rather larger payments system, and the savings denominated in it, were treated as the sort of thing a theory could break and replace. The echo is not an identity. It is a question about who is permitted a gradualism, and on what grounds. The question is better left standing than answered with a sneer.

Two echoes from the present, and no more. Javier Milei's Argentina is a conscious heir of the stabilisation shock, in a country that already has markets, which puts it nearer La Paz than Moscow. Whether it earns the Bolivian result is a question for a later ledger, not for this one. Greek austerity was not shock therapy, and conscripting it into the story is a way of winning an argument by expanding a definition until it covers the grievance in front of you. China's unfinished reform of state firms is the bill that dual-track pricing deferred. The bill is real. It is not a retrospective endorsement of loans-for-shares.

Return, at the end, to the window, and not to the shop. The shop is the easier image, and the less instructive. Extraordinary politics was a true description of a short season in several countries. It described, accurately, the months in which a public will accept what it will not accept once parties, unions, and newspapers have recovered their voice. It was a poor description of how a market society is built. That building takes courts, habits of payment, a central bank with a single sovereign, and a public that can lose an election without losing the regime. None of these arrives in a decree, and none of them is optional.

Shock therapy solved the problem it was designed for more often than the problem it was asked to solve. It was designed to stop a monetary chaos and to end a queue. Asked to found a capitalism, it could end the queue and, where the central bank was actually captured, stop the chaos. The rest — owners who invest rather than strip, a state that can enforce a contract, a recovery that does not wait on an oil price or a Brussels timetable — was never in the syringe. The asking was the error. The designing, against a printing press and an empty shelf, was not a crime.
